# Course: The Open Crumb Method
Outcome: Bake a sourdough loaf with an open, even crumb, reliably, at home.
Audience: Home bakers who already keep a starter but get dense or gummy loaves.
Methodology: Temperature drives everything. We control dough temperature first, fermentation time second, and shaping third. Bakers who chase hydration before temperature stay stuck.

## Module: Starter Strength
Summary: A loaf can only be as strong as the starter that leavens it.

### Lesson: Reading your starter

#### Step: Feed at one to five to five
Instructions: Discard all but twenty grams of starter. Add one hundred grams of flour and one hundred grams of water at twenty-six degrees celsius. Mix until no dry flour remains. Mark the level with a rubber band.
Expected result: The starter rises to double the marked level in six to eight hours, with a domed top and a smell like yoghurt.
Completion criteria: Two consecutive feeds that double within eight hours.
Problem: The starter never doubles
  Cause: The kitchen is below twenty-two degrees, so fermentation stalls.
  Fix: Move it to the oven with only the light on, and feed again. Check the dough temperature with a thermometer rather than guessing.
Problem: It doubles then collapses before I get to it
  Cause: It is peaking earlier than the feed schedule assumes.
  Fix: This is a strong starter, not a problem. Use it at peak — set a timer for five hours and check from then.
Problem: It smells like nail polish remover
  Cause: Underfed, so it has run out of food and gone acidic.
  Fix: Feed twice a day at one to five to five for three days before baking with it.

#### Step: The float test
Instructions: Drop a teaspoon of starter into room temperature water at peak rise.
Expected result: It floats and holds together.
Completion criteria: It floats on two consecutive days.
Problem: It sinks every time
  Cause: The starter is being tested past its peak, when the gas has escaped.
  Fix: Test it at the highest point of the rise, not the next morning.

## Module: Bulk Fermentation
Summary: Where open crumb is won or lost.

### Lesson: Hitting dough temperature

#### Step: Calculate your water temperature
Instructions: Take the desired dough temperature of twenty-five degrees, multiply by three, then subtract your flour temperature, your room temperature, and your starter temperature. The result is the water temperature to use.
Expected result: The dough measures within one degree of twenty-five degrees straight after mixing.
Completion criteria: Three bakes in a row landing within one degree.
Problem: My dough comes out at twenty-one degrees
  Cause: Cold flour, usually flour stored in an unheated room or a garage.
  Fix: Measure the flour temperature rather than assuming it matches the room, and recalculate. Warm water is doing more work than you think.
Problem: The dough is at twenty-eight degrees and moving fast
  Cause: Water was too warm for the room.
  Fix: Cut the bulk time by a third and watch the dough rather than the clock. Next bake, recalculate with the real room temperature.

#### Step: Judge the end of bulk
Instructions: Watch for a rise of about fifty percent, a domed surface, and bubbles at the edge of the container. Do not go by time alone.
Expected result: The dough is jiggly, holds a shape when tipped out, and has visible bubbles under the surface.
Completion criteria: The dough passes the poke test — an indent springs back slowly and halfway.
Problem: My dough is soupy and spreads out flat
  Cause: Bulk went too long at too high a temperature.
  Fix: End bulk at fifty percent rise, not double. At twenty-seven degrees or above, start checking two hours earlier than the recipe says.
Problem: The crumb is dense and tight after baking
  Cause: Bulk ended too early — the dough was underfermented.
  Fix: Extend bulk until the dough rises fifty percent and feels airy. Keep the dough at twenty-five degrees so the timing is predictable.

## Module: Shaping and Bake
Summary: Preserving the gas you spent all day building.

### Lesson: Final shaping

#### Step: Pre-shape and rest
Instructions: Tip the dough out, fold it into a loose round with a bench scraper, and rest it uncovered for twenty to thirty minutes.
Expected result: The round holds its shape and spreads only slightly.
Completion criteria: The surface is smooth and slightly taut before final shaping.
Problem: The dough spreads into a puddle during the rest
  Cause: Either the dough is overfermented, or the pre-shape was too loose.
  Fix: Pre-shape a second time with more tension and shorten the rest to fifteen minutes. If it still spreads, the problem is back in bulk.

#### Step: Bake with steam
Instructions: Bake at two hundred and fifty degrees in a covered dutch oven for twenty minutes, then uncovered at two hundred and thirty for twenty-five minutes.
Expected result: A loaf that springs open at the score, with a deep brown crust.
Completion criteria: The internal temperature reads at least ninety-six degrees.
Problem: The crust is pale and soft
  Cause: The oven is running cooler than the dial says.
  Fix: Use an oven thermometer and extend the uncovered stage by five to ten minutes.
Problem: The loaf is gummy inside
  Cause: It was cut before it finished setting.
  Fix: Cool completely on a rack for at least four hours before slicing. This is not underbaking in most cases.

## Reference: Why temperature before hydration
Body: Most bakers raise hydration to chase an open crumb and end up with slack dough they cannot shape. Dough temperature changes fermentation rate far more than hydration changes crumb structure. Fix temperature first, hold hydration at seventy percent, and only raise it once bulk is predictable.

## Reference: Common questions about starters
Body: A starter kept in the fridge needs two feeds at room temperature before it can leaven a loaf. Discard is not waste; it can be used in pancakes. A layer of dark liquid on top means the starter is hungry, not dead.
